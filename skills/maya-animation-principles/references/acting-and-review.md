# Acting and shot review

Use the user's direction, reference, and shot context to decide what the character
is trying to do and what changes during the performance. This guide supplies
review questions and edit choices, not prescribed acting poses.

## Intention and staging

Give a beat an active intention: conceal uncertainty, persuade someone, inspect a
threat, or resist an impulse. Identify what causes the next thought or action.
Avoid substituting a generic expression or constant motion for that causal change.

Judge the shot camera first. Check silhouette, eyeline, hand visibility, overlaps,
screen direction, and the viewer's likely focus. Pose the torso, head, and hands
so the intended action is legible at the actual shot size. Close-ups diagnose
detail; the full shot determines whether that detail matters to the audience.

Appeal can come from specificity, contrast, clarity, and personality. Preserve
asymmetry when it communicates attitude. A villain, exhausted worker, or awkward
character need not become cute or conventionally graceful to become engaging.

## Gaze, thought, and gesture

Choose a believable focus and stimulus for gaze changes. Eyes may lead a head or
body turn when attention motivates it; a recoil or externally forced movement can
have a different order. Avoid imposing an eyes-first sequence on every action.

Inspect eye aim, lids, brows, head, chest, and hands as one performance. Place
gesture accents where they clarify intention. Vary their size and timing; repeated
equally strong accents can make distinct thoughts feel mechanically uniform.

During a hold, decide what remains active: gaze, breath, grip pressure, or posture.
Use only movement that supports the thought. A deliberate still hold is valid.
Random micro-motion and compulsory blinks can weaken attention and emotional weight.

## Dialogue and reference

Read or listen to the supplied performance when the tools permit it. A transcript
does not provide delivery, breaths, pauses, or emphasis. If audio has not been
heard or reference frames have not been inspected, say so rather than inventing
an interpretation from a filename.

Keep scene FPS, reference FPS, and the source-to-scene offset explicit. Locate
important pauses and stressed moments, then test gesture and facial timing against
the actual delivery. Avoid automatically hitting every syllable with body motion.
Match the rig's available mouth and facial controls to the observed performance;
do not infer a facial-control vocabulary from unknown node names.

Use references to understand force, rhythm, and intention. Adapt for differences
in anatomy, staging, and stylization. Favor comparisons of poses and beats over
pixel matching across different characters or cameras.

## Review and communicate

Start with the largest issue affecting the user's goal. Check intent and staging,
then relevant weight/contact problems and timing, then arcs/overlap and fine detail.
This is a prioritization aid, not a mandatory full-shot pass for every small edit.

Use a concrete review note, such as:

> Frames 30-38: the reaching hand hides against the chest, weakening the decision
> to stop. Move the elbow slightly out in the shot view, preserve the wrist's
> contact, and compare the silhouette before changing the timing.

This is an original example. Frame numbers illustrate note structure, not a
recipe for a performance. Tie notes to the actual observed interval and control.

For a candidate, explain which visible choice changed and why. Compare under the
same camera and timing basis, or explicitly label beat-aligned comparisons.
Check the transition into and out of the edited range. Numerical improvements
such as reduced drift support review; they do not determine emotional clarity.

Respect accepted poses and feedback. Stop once the requested improvement is
visible and the affected motion has been checked. Offer further exploration only
when a material artistic choice remains unresolved.
